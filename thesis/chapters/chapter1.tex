\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:intro}

Machine learning models are only as trustworthy as the data they are trained
on. Modern image classifiers learn from hundreds of thousands to billions of
samples collected from the web, from crowd workers or from several institutions
at once, and no single party is able to inspect every sample. The training set
has therefore become an attack surface. In a \emph{data poisoning} attack, an
adversary who controls a small fraction of the training data manipulates the
behaviour of every model that is later trained on
it~\cite{biggio2012poisoning,goldblum2023dataset}. This thesis studies a defense
against the most practical form of such attacks, backdoor poisoning, in the
setting where defending against it is hardest: when the training data is
confidential and can only be processed inside secure multi-party computation
(MPC).

\section{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:intro-background}

In a backdoor attack, the adversary adds a \emph{trigger} to a few percent of
the training images and relabels them to a target class of their choice. The
trigger can be a small visible patch~\cite{gu2019badnets}, a faint pattern
blended into the whole image~\cite{chen2017blended} or an imperceptible
geometric warping~\cite{nguyen2021wanet}. A model trained on the poisoned set
behaves normally on clean inputs, so its accuracy on a held-out test set barely
changes, but it predicts the target class whenever the trigger is present.
Because standard validation metrics do not reveal the backdoor, it can stay
unnoticed until the adversary activates it. Backdoor learning has grown into a
research field of its own~\cite{li2024backdoorsurvey}, while interviews with
28 organisations revealed that industry practitioners lack the tools to
protect, detect and respond to attacks on their machine learning
systems~\cite{kumar2020industry}. The threat is also practical
at scale: Carlini \emph{et al.}~\cite{carlini2024webscale} showed that an
attacker could have poisoned 0.01\% of two popular web-scale image--text
datasets for about 60~USD by purchasing expired domains referenced by the
datasets.

The data that is most valuable for training is often the data that cannot be
shared. Hospitals, banks and insurers hold medical images, transactions and
claims that privacy regulation and commercial confidentiality forbid them to
pool in the clear. Secure multi-party computation resolves this conflict: it
allows several parties to compute a function of their joint data so that no
party learns anything beyond the output~\cite{yao1986generate,goldreich1987play,benor1988completeness}.
Over the last decade, MPC protocols for machine learning have progressed from
linear models to the training of convolutional neural
networks~\cite{mohassel2017secureml,mohassel2018aby3,wagh2021falcon,knott2021crypten},
and privacy-preserving deep learning on multi-institutional medical data has
been demonstrated end-to-end~\cite{kaissis2021endtoend}.

MPC, however, protects the confidentiality of the inputs and the correctness of
the computation, not the honesty of the inputs. A member of a data consortium
can contribute poisoned samples just as easily as a web page can, and since the
data is secret-shared, nobody, including the other data owners, can look at it.
Encryption thus removes the last line of defense, namely inspection of the
training data, while leaving the attack surface intact. A poisoning defense for
this setting has to run inside MPC itself, and its cost has to be affordable
there.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:intro-problem}

The strongest dataset purification defenses search for poisoned samples in the
feature space of a neural network that has been trained on the poisoned data.
Spectral Signatures~\cite{tran2018spectral} projects the features onto their
top singular vector, Activation Clustering~\cite{chen2019activation} runs
2-means on them, and later methods replace these statistics with robust
covariance estimation~\cite{hayase2021spectre} or an EM
decomposition~\cite{tang2021scan}. Translated into MPC, every one of these
defenses first requires training a deep network on secret-shared data, which is
the most expensive computation one can ask an MPC engine to perform. Their
statistics then rely on singular value decomposition, $k$-means clustering,
$k$-nearest neighbours and sorting. These operations consist of data-dependent
comparisons and iterations, which are the most expensive primitives of secret
sharing~\cite{hamada2012sorting,mohassel2020kmeans}. State-of-the-art
purification is therefore impractical exactly where inspection of the data is
impossible and a defense is needed most. Moreover, Qi
\emph{et al.}~\cite{qi2023revisiting} have demonstrated that adaptive attacks can
break the latent separability assumption on which these defenses rest.

Perceptual hash functions offer a different trade-off. A perceptual hash maps
an image to a short bit string so that visually similar images obtain similar
strings~\cite{zauner2010phash,farid2021overview}. Most classical constructions
apply a fixed linear transform, such as resizing, a discrete cosine transform or
a wavelet transform, and then binarise the resulting coefficients. Under linear
secret sharing, a public linear map is computed locally by every party without
any communication, so only the binarisation step costs interaction. Perceptual
hashes are therefore a natural candidate for a cheap, model-free summary of
training images inside MPC.

So far, however, perceptual hashes have entered machine learning security only
as the target of evasion and collision
attacks~\cite{struppek2022neuralhash,jain2022adversarial,prokos2023squint} and
have never been studied as a filter for poisoned training data. There is also a
conceptual tension in such a use: a perceptual hash is designed to be invariant
to small perturbations, and an invisible trigger is precisely a small
perturbation. Three questions therefore remain open: 1)~whether the few
hundred bits that survive a perceptual hash carry enough information to
separate poisoned samples from clean ones; 2)~which kinds of triggers survive
the hash and which are erased by it; and 3)~how much a hash-based defense costs
inside MPC compared with model-based defenses. These unknowns constitute the
research gap that this thesis addresses.

\section{Research Questions and Hypotheses}
\label{sec:intro-rq}

The purpose of this thesis is to determine whether perceptual hash functions
can serve as a model-free defense against backdoor poisoning that is affordable
under MPC. The study addresses three research questions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*}, leftmargin=*]
  \item How effective are perceptual hash functions, combined with detection
    statistics computed on their bits, at removing samples poisoned by
    state-of-the-art backdoor attacks?
  \item Which properties of a trigger determine whether it can be detected from
    per-sample hash bits?
  \item What is the communication and round cost of hash-based dataset
    purification under secret-sharing MPC protocols, and how does it compare
    with the cost of model-based purification defenses?
\end{enumerate}

To answer these questions, the thesis pursues the following objectives:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*)]
  \item to express classical perceptual hashes in a common form, a binarisation
    of a public linear map, and to validate them against reference
    implementations;
  \item to design a family of hashes that targets the high-frequency component
    of an image, where invisible triggers reside, at the same MPC cost as a
    classical hash;
  \item to develop model-free detection statistics and threshold rules that
    operate on hash bits alone;
  \item to evaluate the resulting defense against standard and adaptive
    backdoor attacks, both on the level of detection and on the level of a model
    trained on the purified data;
  \item to estimate the cost of every hash and of the complete purification
    pipeline under three-party and two-party MPC protocols and to compare it
    with model-based defenses;
  \item to release the defense as a module of an open-source backdoor learning
    toolbox.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research Approach}
\label{sec:intro-approach}

The study adopts a strict threat model. The adversary is poison-only: they
modify a part of the training set, cannot influence the training procedure and
know the defense, including the hash functions and the detection statistic. The
defender sees only the secret-shared training set; they have no clean held-out
data, no trained model and no knowledge of the trigger, the target class or the
poisoning rate. This is the only setting in which the cost of a defense does
not depend on training a model, and therefore the only one in which a
deployment inside MPC is plausible.

The research combines analytical and experimental methods. On the analytical
side, every hash is written as $\mathrm{binarise}(Lx)$ with a public matrix $L$,
which makes it possible to reason about which triggers a hash can see and to
derive its MPC cost from the binarisation rule alone. On the experimental side,
26 hash functions and seven detection statistics are evaluated on the CIFAR-10
dataset~\cite{krizhevsky2009cifar} against 13 poisoning attacks, eight of
which are taken from the BackdoorBox toolbox~\cite{li2023backdoorbox}. A
label-flip-only control, which relabels samples without adding any trigger,
separates genuine trigger detection from the detection of label noise. The
effect of purification on the trained model is measured with a
ResNet-18~\cite{he2016resnet} through its benign accuracy and attack success
rate. Finally, every hash is executed on an instrumented fixed-point
secret-sharing type, checked bit by bit against its plaintext version, and its
communication primitives are counted and priced under a three-party replicated
protocol~\cite{araki2016highthroughput,mohassel2018aby3} and a two-party
SPDZ-style protocol~\cite{damgard2012spdz,cramer2018spdz2k}.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:intro-contributions}

The novelty of the thesis lies in the following contributions.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*)]
  \item The thesis presents the first systematic study of perceptual hash
    functions as a defense against training-data poisoning.
  \item It shows that every classical perceptual hash except the colour hash can be
    written as a binarisation of a public linear map, so that the MPC cost of a
    hash is determined by its binarisation rule alone, and it derives cheaper
    binarisation rules with the same detection power.
  \item It proposes a family of residual hashes, a perceptual complement that
    binarises exactly the high-frequency component a perceptual hash discards,
    within the same MPC cost class.
  \item It characterises theoretically which triggers can be detected from hash
    bits: invariance of a hash implies blindness to triggers in its null space,
    and sample-specific triggers are undetectable from per-sample summaries.
  \item It introduces an evaluation protocol with a label-flip-only control and
    shows that the number of poisoned samples that survive purification, rather
    than the share of poison removed, determines whether a backdoor survives.
  \item It provides an open-source implementation, including an MPC cost tracer
    and an integration into BackdoorBox.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Main Results}
\label{sec:intro-results}

The study found that classical perceptual hashes are largely ineffective as a
poisoning defense. Only the hashes based on the discrete cosine transform
detected fixed patch triggers, while all classical hashes were blind to
invisible triggers, as their invariance predicts. The proposed residual hashes,
in contrast, detected every sample-agnostic trigger that was tested, including
invisible blended, steganographic and adaptive triggers, and removed 64--100\%
of the poisoned samples at the cost of 1--14\% of the clean data. As
hypothesised, no hash detected sample-specific triggers such as WaNet and
Refool. On a trained ResNet-18, purification reduced the attack success rate
from 1.00 to 0.00 for the adaptive attack and from 1.00 to 0.15 for the blended
attack, while benign accuracy stayed within 1.2 percentage points of the
undefended model. For BadNets, however, removing 64\% of the poison left 906
poisoned samples in the training set, and the backdoor survived unchanged.

Under MPC, the linear part of every hash was indeed free. Purifying the
50\,000 images of the CIFAR-10 training set required about 29~GB of
communication and 78~s on a local network under the three-party protocol, which
is about $2\cdot10^{9}$ times less than a model-based defense and 326 times less
than $k$-nearest neighbours in pixel space. These results suggest that
perceptual hashing provides a cheap, model-free filter that eliminates
sample-agnostic poison-only backdoors and forces the adversary into the
sample-specific regime, provided that the filter is tuned for near-total recall.

\section{Thesis Outline}
\label{sec:intro-outline}

The remainder of the thesis is organised as follows.
Chapter~\ref{chap:lr} reviews the literature on backdoor attacks and defenses,
perceptual hashing and secure multi-party computation for machine learning, and
identifies the gap that this work fills. Chapter~\ref{chap:met} sets out the
methodology: the threat model, the normal form of a perceptual hash, the
residual hash family, the detection statistics and threshold rules, and the MPC
cost model. Chapter~\ref{chap:impl} describes the implementation of the hash
library, the poisoned datasets, the secret-sharing tracer and the BackdoorBox
module. Chapter~\ref{chap:eval} presents and discusses the experimental results,
including their limitations. Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} concludes the thesis
and outlines directions for future work.
