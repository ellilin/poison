\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{chap:lr}

This thesis connects four lines of research that have so far developed largely
independently of each other: data poisoning and backdoor attacks, defenses
against them, perceptual hash functions, and secure multi-party computation for
machine learning. This chapter reviews each of them in turn.
Section~\ref{sec:lr-attacks} classifies backdoor attacks and the metrics used
to evaluate them. Section~\ref{sec:lr-defenses} surveys backdoor defenses, with
an emphasis on dataset purification, the family to which the defense studied in
this thesis belongs. Section~\ref{sec:lr-phash} introduces perceptual hash
functions, their classical constructions and their known weaknesses.
Section~\ref{sec:lr-mpc} reviews MPC protocols for machine learning, their cost
structure and the existing work on poisoning in privacy-preserving learning.
Every method is evaluated against the setting of this thesis: a poison-only
adversary and a defender who can access the training data only in
secret-shared form. Section~\ref{sec:lr-summary} summarises the review,
identifies the research gap and justifies the choice of the research method.

\section{Data Poisoning and Backdoor Attacks}
\label{sec:lr-attacks}

\subsection{Threat Models}
\label{sec:lr-threat}

Poisoning attacks on machine learning have been studied at least since Biggio
\emph{et al.}~\cite{biggio2012poisoning} showed that a few carefully crafted
training points could substantially increase the test error of a support vector
machine. The surveys by Goldblum \emph{et al.}~\cite{goldblum2023dataset} and
Li \emph{et al.}~\cite{li2024backdoorsurvey} distinguish poisoning attacks by
their goal. \emph{Availability} attacks degrade the accuracy of the model on all
inputs and are therefore easy to notice. \emph{Targeted} attacks change the
prediction on specific test inputs; Shafahi
\emph{et al.}~\cite{shafahi2018poisonfrogs}, for example, made a chosen test
image misclassified by adding a few correctly labelled but adversarially
perturbed images to the training set. \emph{Backdoor} attacks are the most
insidious: the poisoned model keeps its accuracy on clean inputs and changes its
prediction only on inputs that contain an adversary-chosen trigger. Since a
backdoor is invisible to standard validation, this thesis focuses on backdoor
attacks.

The literature characterises backdoor attacks along several axes. Four of them
determine whether a data-level defense can work in principle.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*)]
  \item \emph{Adversary capability.} A \emph{poison-only} adversary can only
    modify the training data, whereas a \emph{training-controlled} adversary
    also controls the training procedure, the loss or the training code. A
    filter applied to the training data is meaningful only against the former,
    because the latter can produce a backdoored model regardless of what the
    data looks like.
  \item \emph{Label consistency.} \emph{Dirty-label} attacks relabel the poisoned
    samples to the target class. \emph{Clean-label} attacks poison only samples
    of the target class and keep their correct labels, which defeats every
    defense that looks for mislabelled samples.
  \item \emph{Visibility.} A trigger may be clearly visible, such as a patch, or
    invisible, with a perturbation small enough to go unnoticed by a human
    inspector.
  \item \emph{Trigger locality.} A \emph{sample-agnostic} trigger adds the same
    pattern to every poisoned sample, whereas a \emph{sample-specific} trigger
    produces a different perturbation for every image.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Representative Attacks}
\label{sec:lr-attack-list}

Table~\ref{tab:lr-attacks} classifies the attacks discussed in this section
along the four axes. The first backdoor attack, BadNets, was introduced by Gu
\emph{et al.}~\cite{gu2019badnets}. It stamped a small fixed patch, for example
a $3\times3$ white square, onto a fraction of the training images and relabelled
them to the target class. Chen \emph{et al.}~\cite{chen2017blended} replaced
the patch with a full-image pattern blended into the image with weight
$\alpha$; as $\alpha$ decreases, the blended trigger becomes invisible while it
remains learnable. These two attacks remain the standard baselines of every
backdoor benchmark~\cite{li2023backdoorbox,wu2022backdoorbench}. TrojanNN by
Liu \emph{et al.}~\cite{liu2018trojaning} generated a trigger that maximally
activated selected neurons of a pre-trained model and retrained the model,
which requires access to the model rather than only to the data.

Clean-label attacks emerged as a response to the observation that relabelled
samples are easy to spot. Turner \emph{et al.}~\cite{turner2019labelconsistent}
first made target-class images hard to classify with adversarial perturbations
and then added a visible patch, so that the network had to rely on the patch.
Saha \emph{et al.}~\cite{saha2020hidden} hid the trigger completely at training
time: their poisoned images looked like correctly labelled target-class images
but were close to patched images in feature space. Zhao
\emph{et al.}~\cite{zhao2020cleanlabelvideo} extended clean-label attacks to
video recognition with a universal adversarial trigger, and Souri
\emph{et al.}~\cite{souri2022sleeper} used gradient matching to obtain hidden
trigger backdoors that survived training from scratch. Refool by Liu
\emph{et al.}~\cite{liu2020refool} used physically plausible reflections as
triggers and was also designed as a clean-label attack.

A third line of work makes the trigger invisible and different for every
sample. WaNet~\cite{nguyen2021wanet} applies a smooth elastic warping field
instead of adding a pattern, so the pixel-level change depends on the image
content; its original version also modifies the training procedure with a
``noise mode'' to evade defenses. ISSBA by Li
\emph{et al.}~\cite{li2021issba} used a trained encoder to embed the same
string into every poisoned image, which produced an invisible, sample-specific
additive residual. Input-aware dynamic attacks~\cite{nguyen2020iad} and
LIRA~\cite{doan2021lira} went further and learned the trigger generator jointly
with the model, and blind backdoors~\cite{bagdasaryan2021blind} compromised the
loss computation code itself. These three attacks are training-controlled and
therefore outside the scope of any data filter. BATT by Xu
\emph{et al.}~\cite{xu2023batt} took a different direction and used a spatial
transformation, such as a fixed rotation, as the trigger, so that no additive
pattern exists at all.

The frequency domain offers another view of triggers. Zeng
\emph{et al.}~\cite{zeng2021frequency} show that many existing triggers leave
severe high-frequency artefacts, which allow poisoned inputs to be detected
without knowledge of the attack or the target model, and propose smooth
low-frequency triggers to evade such detection. Feng
\emph{et al.}~\cite{feng2022fiba} inject the low-frequency content of a trigger
image into the amplitude spectrum of medical images, a domain that is
particularly relevant to privacy-preserving training.

Finally, Qi \emph{et al.}~\cite{qi2023revisiting} designed adaptive attacks
against a whole family of defenses. Most defenses assume that a model trained on
poisoned data maps poisoned and clean samples to separable clusters in its
latent space. Qi \emph{et al.} combined several low-opacity triggers with
``cover'' samples that carry the trigger but keep their correct label, and
showed that the resulting poisoned samples no longer formed a separable
cluster. Since then, an evaluation against adaptive attacks has become a
necessary part of proposing a new defense.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:lr-metrics}

The metrics used in the literature fall into three groups: model-level metrics,
which describe the trained model; data-level metrics, which describe how well a
purification defense separates poisoned samples from clean ones; and cost
metrics, which are specific to the MPC setting and are discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:lr-mpc-cost}.

At the model level, an attack is evaluated with two
quantities~\cite{li2024backdoorsurvey,li2023backdoorbox}. Let $f$ be the trained
classifier, $D_{\mathrm{test}}$ the clean test set, $t$ the target class and
$T$ the trigger function. The \emph{benign accuracy} (BA) is the accuracy on
clean test data, and the \emph{attack success rate} (ASR) is the share of test
samples outside the target class that are classified as $t$ once the trigger is
applied:
\begin{align}
\mathrm{BA}  &= \frac{1}{|D_{\mathrm{test}}|}\sum_{(x,y)\in D_{\mathrm{test}}} \mathbb{I}\left[f(x)=y\right], \label{eq:ba}\\
\mathrm{ASR} &= \frac{1}{|D_{\mathrm{test}}^{\neg t}|}\sum_{(x,y)\in D_{\mathrm{test}}^{\neg t}} \mathbb{I}\left[f(T(x))=t\right], \label{eq:asr}
\end{align}
where $\mathbb{I}[\cdot]$ is the indicator function and
$D_{\mathrm{test}}^{\neg t}=\{(x,y)\in D_{\mathrm{test}} : y\neq t\}$. A
successful attack keeps BA close to that of a clean model and drives ASR close
to one; a successful defense keeps BA and drives ASR to zero.

At the data level, a purification defense assigns a suspicion score to every
training sample and removes the samples whose score exceeds a threshold. The
quality of the score, independent of the threshold, is measured with the area
under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC)~\cite{fawcett2006roc},
which equals the probability that a randomly chosen poisoned sample receives a
higher score than a randomly chosen clean one. The quality of the final
decision is measured by the true and false positive rates. Let $S$ be the set of
poisoned samples among the $n$ training samples and $K$ the set of samples kept
by the defense. In this thesis, the two rates are called the \emph{elimination
rate} $\eta$ and the \emph{sacrifice rate} $\sigma$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:eta-sigma}
\eta = \frac{|S\setminus K|}{|S|}, \qquad
\sigma = \frac{|\overline{K}\setminus S|}{n-|S|},
\end{equation}
where $\overline{K}$ is the set of removed samples. The elimination rate is the
share of poison removed, and the sacrifice rate is the share of clean data lost.

\section{Backdoor Defenses}
\label{sec:lr-defenses}

\subsection{Taxonomy}
\label{sec:lr-def-taxonomy}

Following the taxonomy of Li \emph{et al.}~\cite{li2024backdoorsurvey} and of
the BackdoorBox toolbox~\cite{li2023backdoorbox}, backdoor defenses can be
grouped by the stage of the machine learning pipeline at which they act.
Table~\ref{tab:lr-defenses} summarises the groups and the resources that each
of them requires.

\emph{Input pre-processing} defenses try to destroy the trigger before a sample
reaches a deployed model. Liu \emph{et al.}~\cite{liu2017neuraltrojans} passed
inputs through an autoencoder, and ShrinkPad~\cite{li2021shrinkpad} shrinks and
pads the input image to break the spatial alignment of a trigger. Such defenses
mitigate the backdoor at inference time but do not prevent it from being
learned.

\emph{Model repair} defenses remove the backdoor from a trained model.
Fine-Pruning~\cite{liu2018fineprune} pruned neurons that were dormant on clean
data and then fine-tuned the model, mode connectivity
repair~\cite{zhao2020mcr} searched for a path between two models in the loss
landscape, and Neural Attention Distillation~\cite{li2021nad} fine-tuned a
teacher model on a small clean set and distilled its attention maps into the
backdoored student. Neural Cleanse~\cite{wang2019neuralcleanse} reverse-engineered
a minimal trigger for every class and flagged classes with anomalously small
triggers. All of these methods require a trained model and a reserved set of
clean data.

\emph{Poison suppression} defenses modify the training procedure itself.
Anti-Backdoor Learning~\cite{li2021abl} exploited the observation that poisoned
samples are learned faster than clean ones, isolated the samples with the lowest
loss early in training and then unlearned them. Huang
\emph{et al.}~\cite{huang2022dbd} decoupled training into self-supervised
learning of the feature extractor, supervised training of the classifier head
and semi-supervised fine-tuning, which prevents the trigger from being tied to
the target label.

\emph{Input-level detection} defenses decide at inference time whether a query
carries a trigger. STRIP~\cite{gao2019strip} superimposed the input with clean
images and flagged inputs whose predictions had suspiciously low entropy.
SCALE-UP~\cite{guo2023scaleup} amplifies pixel values and
IBD-PSC~\cite{hou2024ibdpsc} scales the parameters of batch normalisation layers;
both rely on the observation that predictions on poisoned inputs stay
consistent under such amplification. Each query thus costs several forward
passes of the model.

\emph{Certified} defenses provide provable guarantees against a bounded number
of poisoned samples. Steinhardt \emph{et al.}~\cite{steinhardt2017certified}
bounded the loss that an attacker could cause against defenses based on outlier
removal, and Deep Partition Aggregation~\cite{levine2021dpa} trains a separate
classifier on each of many disjoint partitions of the training set and takes a
majority vote. The guarantees hold only for a small absolute number of poisoned
samples and come at the cost of training many models.

\emph{Dataset purification} defenses, finally, remove poisoned samples from the
training set before the final model is trained. They are reviewed in detail in
the next subsection, because the defense studied in this thesis belongs to this
family.

\subsection{Dataset Purification}
\label{sec:lr-purification}

Dataset purification is the only family that removes the poison before the
backdoor is learned and that requires no clean data, which makes it the natural
choice when the training data comes from untrusted contributors. The
foundational method, Spectral Signatures by Tran
\emph{et al.}~\cite{tran2018spectral}, trained a network on the poisoned data,
computed the covariance of the penultimate-layer representations within each
class and scored every sample by its squared projection onto the top singular
vector; the $1.5\varepsilon$ fraction of the highest-scoring samples was
removed, where $\varepsilon$ is an upper bound on the poisoning rate. Activation
Clustering by Chen \emph{et al.}~\cite{chen2019activation} reduced the
last-layer activations of each class to a few dimensions with independent
component analysis and split them into two clusters with $k$-means; a
suspiciously small or well-separated cluster was declared poisoned.

Later methods strengthened the statistic. SPECTRE by Hayase
\emph{et al.}~\cite{hayase2021spectre} whitens the representations with a
robust covariance estimate and scores samples with quantum entropy scoring,
which amplifies a weak spectral signature. SCAn by Tang
\emph{et al.}~\cite{tang2021scan} models the representations of each class as
a mixture of a class identity and a within-class variation and tests with an
EM-based decomposition whether a class contains two subpopulations. The
latent separability assumption behind these methods was then challenged by the
adaptive attacks of Qi \emph{et al.}~\cite{qi2023revisiting}. Subsequent
defenses therefore act on the training process more actively: ASSET by Pan
\emph{et al.}~\cite{pan2023asset} induces different model behaviours on
backdoor and clean samples through an offset optimisation, confusion training by
Qi \emph{et al.}~\cite{qi2023proactive} trains an auxiliary model that fits
only the backdoor correlation, and FLARE by Hou
\emph{et al.}~\cite{hou2025flare} aggregates abnormal activations from all
hidden layers instead of the last one.

Despite their differences, all of these methods share one structural
dependency: their statistic lives in the representation space of a network that
must first be trained on the poisoned data, and it is computed with singular
value decomposition, clustering, EM or robust covariance estimation. As
Section~\ref{sec:lr-mpc-cost} shows, both requirements are among the most
expensive things one can compute under MPC.

The only data-level statistic that does not depend on the victim model is the
frequency analysis of Zeng \emph{et al.}~\cite{zeng2021frequency}. It
demonstrates that the high-frequency component of an image carries much of the
trigger signal, an observation that the residual hashes proposed in this thesis
build upon. Their detector, however, is itself a neural network trained to
recognise trigger artefacts, and it was proposed for inference-time inputs
rather than for purifying a training set.

\section{Perceptual Hash Functions}
\label{sec:lr-phash}

\subsection{Definition and Design Goals}
\label{sec:lr-phash-def}

A perceptual hash function maps an image $x$ to a short bit string
$h(x)\in\{0,1\}^{B}$ so that perceptually similar images obtain similar strings
under the Hamming distance $d_H$, whereas unrelated images obtain strings at a
distance close to $B/2$~\cite{zauner2010phash,farid2021overview}. The two
requirements are usually called \emph{robustness} and
\emph{discrimination}~\cite{monga2006perceptual}. Early work by Venkatesan
\emph{et al.}~\cite{venkatesan2000robust} computed randomised statistics of
wavelet coefficients, Swaminathan \emph{et al.}~\cite{swaminathan2006robust}
studied key-dependent hashes and their security, and Monga and
Evans~\cite{monga2006perceptual} built hashes from robust feature points and
analysed the trade-off between robustness and discrimination. Zauner's
benchmark~\cite{zauner2010phash} established the DCT-based pHash as a practical
standard, and Farid~\cite{farid2021overview} gives a recent overview of the
field. Perceptual hashes are the standard tool for near-duplicate detection,
copyright enforcement and the detection of known illegal content.

\subsection{Classical Constructions}
\label{sec:lr-phash-zoo}

Table~\ref{tab:lr-hashes} lists the constructions that are used in practice.
The simplest ones, the average and difference hashes, are folklore methods
popularised by open-source libraries. pHash applies the discrete cosine
transform (DCT)~\cite{ahmed1974dct} to a downscaled image and keeps the signs
of its low-frequency coefficients relative to their median; the wavelet hash
does the same with the approximation band of a Haar wavelet
transform~\cite{mallat1989wavelet}. The block mean hash of Yang
\emph{et al.}~\cite{yang2006blockmean} thresholds the mean intensities of image
blocks, and the Marr--Hildreth hash~\cite{zauner2010phash} binarises pooled
responses of the Laplacian-of-Gaussian edge detector~\cite{marr1980edge}. PDQ,
released by Meta in 2019~\cite{meta2019pdq}, is a hardened variant of pHash
with a 256-bit output. PhotoDNA and NeuralHash are deployed in industry for
content scanning. PhotoDNA, developed by Microsoft, is proprietary and has only
been described at a high level~\cite{farid2021overview}, whereas NeuralHash
projects the embedding of a convolutional network onto random
hyperplanes~\cite{apple2021neuralhash,struppek2022neuralhash}.

An important observation for this thesis concerns the structure of these
constructions. Apart from the colour hash, PhotoDNA and NeuralHash, each of
them first applies a fixed linear transform, a combination of colour
conversion, resampling, filtering, DCT or wavelet transform and pooling, and
then compares each resulting coefficient with a threshold. Chapter~\ref{chap:met}
makes this observation precise and exploits it for the MPC analysis.

\subsection{Security of Perceptual Hashing}
\label{sec:lr-phash-security}

Perceptual hashes are not cryptographic hash functions: they are designed to
leak similarity, and the security community has repeatedly shown how easily
they can be manipulated. Hao \emph{et al.}~\cite{hao2021notwhat} demonstrated
black-box attacks that changed an image imperceptibly while pushing its hash far
from the original, which subverted perceptual-hash-based image search.
After Apple announced NeuralHash, Struppek
\emph{et al.}~\cite{struppek2022neuralhash} showed that gradient-based attacks
produced both collisions and evasions with imperceptible changes. Jain
\emph{et al.}~\cite{jain2022adversarial} showed that perceptual-hash-based
client-side scanning could be evaded in a black-box setting, and Prokos
\emph{et al.}~\cite{prokos2023squint} mounted collision and detection-avoidance
attacks against PhotoDNA and PDQ. Abelson \emph{et al.}~\cite{abelson2024bugs}
summarised the risks of client-side scanning from a systems and policy
perspective.

The lesson that matters for this thesis is not that perceptual hashes are weak
scanners, but that their invariance cuts both ways. The robustness that makes a
hash map a slightly modified image to the same bits also makes it blind to a
slight modification, and an invisible backdoor trigger is exactly such a
modification. A defense that relies on a perceptual hash must therefore either
accept this blindness or change the hash so that it retains the information that
a trigger leaves behind.

\subsection{Hashing in Machine Learning Pipelines}
\label{sec:lr-phash-ml}

Hashing already plays a role in the curation of training data, but never as a
defense against poisoning. Barz and Denzler~\cite{barz2020cifair} found that
3.3\% and 10\% of the test images of CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 had near-duplicates
in the training set and released a cleaned test set. Near-duplicate removal of
this kind targets redundancy, not adversarial structure. Carlini
\emph{et al.}~\cite{carlini2024webscale} recommended distributing cryptographic
hashes of the content of web-scale datasets to defeat split-view poisoning,
which protects the integrity of exact content rather than detecting poisoned
samples. Perceptual hashes have also been combined with cryptographic
protocols: Kulshrestha and Mayer~\cite{kulshrestha2021identifying} proposed
private membership computation that checks whether the perceptual hash of a
message is close to a hash in a database of known harmful media. These
protocols, however, match images against a known list and do not search for
unknown poisoned samples. To the best of the author's knowledge, perceptual
hashes have appeared in machine learning security only as the object of attacks
and have never been used as a filter for poisoned training data.

\section{Secure Multi-Party Computation for Machine Learning}
\label{sec:lr-mpc}

\subsection{Foundations}
\label{sec:lr-mpc-foundations}

Secure multi-party computation allows $N$ parties holding private inputs to
compute a joint function so that nothing beyond the output is revealed. Yao
introduced garbled circuits for the two-party case~\cite{yao1986generate}, and
Goldreich, Micali and Wigderson~\cite{goldreich1987play} and Ben-Or,
Goldwasser and Wigderson~\cite{benor1988completeness} showed that any function
can be computed securely by several parties, the latter with
information-theoretic security based on Shamir's secret
sharing~\cite{shamir1979share}. Beaver~\cite{beaver1991efficient} showed that
multiplications can be performed cheaply online with correlated randomness
prepared in advance, which is the basis of most modern protocols. Protocols are
distinguished by the corruption model, semi-honest or malicious, and by the
number of corrupted parties they tolerate, an honest majority or a dishonest
majority.

\subsection{Protocols and Frameworks for Machine Learning}
\label{sec:lr-mpc-frameworks}

Two protocol families dominate privacy-preserving machine learning. In the
honest-majority three-party setting, replicated secret sharing over the ring
$\mathbb{Z}_{2^k}$, introduced in a high-throughput form by Araki
\emph{et al.}~\cite{araki2016highthroughput}, underlies ABY3 by Mohassel and
Rindal~\cite{mohassel2018aby3}, SecureNN~\cite{wagh2019securenn} and
Falcon~\cite{wagh2021falcon}. ABY3 combined arithmetic, binary and garbled
sharings with efficient conversions and introduced a local probabilistic
truncation for fixed-point arithmetic. In the dishonest-majority setting,
SPDZ~\cite{damgard2012spdz} used somewhat homomorphic encryption to prepare
multiplication triples and information-theoretic MACs to achieve malicious
security, and SPD$\mathbb{Z}_{2^k}$~\cite{cramer2018spdz2k} carried this
approach over to rings. Two-party mixed-protocol frameworks include
ABY~\cite{demmler2015aby} and SecureML~\cite{mohassel2017secureml}, which
trained linear models and neural networks with two non-colluding servers.
CrypTen~\cite{knott2021crypten} exposes secure computation through a
PyTorch-like interface, and MP-SPDZ~\cite{keller2020mpspdz} implements a large
number of protocol variants behind one high-level language, which makes it the
standard tool for comparing protocols.

\subsection{Cost Structure}
\label{sec:lr-mpc-cost}

Although these protocols differ in their security guarantees, they share a
common cost structure, which Table~\ref{tab:lr-mpc-cost} summarises. Two
measures dominate the cost: the volume of communication and the number of
sequential communication rounds. Over a wide-area network, the latter often
determines the running time.

Addition of shared values and multiplication by a public constant are local
operations in every linear secret-sharing scheme. Consequently, any linear map
with public coefficients, such as a convolution with public weights, a DCT, a
wavelet transform or average pooling, costs no communication at all. A
multiplication of two shared values requires one round of interaction and a
constant number of ring elements per party~\cite{beaver1991efficient}.
Fixed-point arithmetic additionally requires a truncation after every
multiplication, which is interactive in general~\cite{catrina2010fixedpoint}
but can be performed locally with a small probabilistic error in the
three-party replicated setting~\cite{mohassel2018aby3}.

Comparisons are the expensive primitive. Deciding whether a shared value is
positive requires access to its most significant bit, that is, a bit
decomposition or an equivalent protocol~\cite{damgard2006constantrounds,catrina2010primitives}.
Practical implementations evaluate a binary adder circuit, for example a
Kogge--Stone parallel prefix adder~\cite{kogge1973parallel}, which costs
$O(k\log k)$ AND gates and $O(\log k)$ rounds for a $k$-bit ring; mixed
arithmetic--binary preprocessing such as edaBits~\cite{escudero2020edabits}
reduces, but does not remove, this cost. Order statistics are more expensive
still. An oblivious sort of $n$ values with Batcher's
network~\cite{batcher1968sorting} needs $O(n\log^2 n)$ compare-exchange
operations in $O(\log^2 n)$ layers, and shuffle-based protocols reduce the
number of comparisons to $O(n\log n)$ at the price of a secure
shuffle~\cite{hamada2012sorting}. Iterative algorithms such as $k$-means must
fix their number of iterations in advance to avoid leaking information and pay
for distance computations and comparisons in every
iteration~\cite{mohassel2020kmeans}. Division, square roots and exponentials
are computed with iterative approximations that require many
rounds~\cite{catrina2010fixedpoint}.

This cost structure has direct consequences for backdoor defenses. Training a
deep network under MPC requires millions of secure multiplications and
comparisons per sample and epoch, which is why secure training remains orders of
magnitude slower than training in the clear even in the most efficient
frameworks~\cite{wagh2021falcon,knott2021crypten}. The model-based purification
defenses of Section~\ref{sec:lr-purification} require such a training run before
the final one and then add SVD, clustering or EM on top of it. A defense whose
arithmetic is a public linear map followed by a fixed number of comparisons per
sample, in contrast, would cost almost nothing beyond those comparisons.

\subsection{Poisoning in Privacy-Preserving Learning}
\label{sec:lr-mpc-poisoning}

The conflict between confidentiality and the ability to inspect training data
was first studied in federated learning. Bagdasaryan
\emph{et al.}~\cite{bagdasaryan2020backdoorfl} showed that a single malicious
participant could replace the global model with a backdoored one, and secure
aggregation~\cite{bonawitz2017secagg}, which hides individual updates from the
server, makes such manipulations harder to detect. Several works have
therefore combined secure aggregation with robustness checks. BREA by So
\emph{et al.}~\cite{so2021brea} integrated verifiable outlier detection into
secure aggregation, FLAME by Nguyen \emph{et al.}~\cite{nguyen2022flame}
clustered model updates and clipped and noised them to eliminate backdoors, and
RoFL~\cite{lycklama2023rofl} and ELSA~\cite{rathee2023elsa} enforced bounds on
the norms of client updates inside the secure protocol. Prio by Corrigan-Gibbs
and Boneh~\cite{corrigangibbs2017prio} validated client inputs with
secret-shared non-interactive proofs before aggregation. Notably, these systems deliberately
restrict themselves to cheap statistics such as norms and avoid medians, sorting
and clustering for the same cost reasons that were discussed above.

For collaborative training in MPC, Chaudhari
\emph{et al.}~\cite{chaudhari2023safenet} proposed SafeNet. In SafeNet, every
data owner trains a model on their own data, the models are combined into an
ensemble inside MPC, and a majority vote limits the influence of a minority of
poisoned owners. This approach is effective and cheap, but it requires every
owner to hold enough data to train a model and it protects against poisoned
owners rather than against poisoned samples.

All of these defenses operate on model updates or on whole models. None of them
filters individual training samples, and none of them applies when the poison is
spread across the data of honest owners, for example because it was scraped from
the web.

The review leads to three conclusions. First, every dataset purification
defense that currently defines the state of the art depends on a model trained
on the poisoned data and on statistics such as SVD, clustering and EM. Both are
among the most expensive computations under MPC, so these defenses are
impractical exactly in the setting where the data cannot be inspected. Second,
the defenses that do exist for privacy-preserving learning operate on model
updates or whole models and do not filter individual samples. Third, perceptual
hash functions fit the MPC cost model almost perfectly, since their arithmetic
is a public linear map and only the final binarisation requires interaction,
but they have never been evaluated as a defense against poisoning, and their
designed invariance suggests that they may be blind to invisible triggers.
Nobody has measured whether the information that survives a perceptual hash is
sufficient to separate poisoned samples from clean ones.

These conclusions justify the research method adopted in the following
chapters. The defense is built on per-sample hash bits and on detection
statistics that need no trained model, so that its MPC cost consists of a fixed
number of comparisons per sample. Because the invariance of classical hashes is
expected to hide invisible triggers, the study also designs hashes that retain
the high-frequency component in which, according to Zeng
\emph{et al.}~\cite{zeng2021frequency}, many triggers reside. The defense is
evaluated with the metrics established in the literature: the detection AUC and
the elimination and sacrifice rates at the data level, and BA and ASR at the
model level. To separate trigger detection from the detection of the label
noise that every dirty-label attack introduces, a label-flip-only control is
added. The attacks are taken from
BackdoorBox~\cite{li2023backdoorbox} and cover all four axes of
Table~\ref{tab:lr-attacks}, including the adaptive attacks of Qi
\emph{et al.}~\cite{qi2023revisiting}, and the experiments use the CIFAR-10
dataset~\cite{krizhevsky2009cifar}, the most common benchmark of the backdoor
literature~\cite{wu2022backdoorbench}. Finally, the MPC cost is estimated by
counting the communication primitives of every hash and pricing them under an
honest-majority three-party replicated protocol~\cite{mohassel2018aby3} and a
dishonest-majority two-party SPDZ-style protocol~\cite{cramer2018spdz2k}, the
two protocol families that dominate privacy-preserving machine learning.
